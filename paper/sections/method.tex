\subsection{Token candidate pool construction}
\label{sec:method:candidates}
Token selection draws candidate tokens from concepts in SNOMED~CT, but not
every concept in the graph is a useful candidate for representing the
mapped concepts $M$. A concept many relationships away from every concept
in $M$ contributes little even when reachable, and searching over it
anyway only adds cost. A concept that is not reachable from $M$ at all by
following relationships forward cannot represent it under our
construction: since \texttt{IS\_A} edges point from specific to general and
attribute edges point from a concept to its attribute value, following
outgoing edges only ever reaches a generalization or an attribute value of
a mapped concept, never something more specific than it. We therefore
restrict candidates to a bounded neighborhood of $M$, the \emph{candidate
pool}.

A single depth budget $D$ plays three roles in this method: it bounds how
far a candidate token may be from the mapped concepts it can help
represent, it bounds how deep a context tree
(Section~\ref{sec:method:tokenizer}) may grow, and it bounds the horizon
over which the coverage score below is computed. Concretely, the candidate
pool is
\[
V_D = M \cup \{\, u \in V : u \text{ is reachable from some } c \in M
\text{ by at most } D \text{ relationships} \,\},
\]
obtained in practice as the union of the $D$-hop ego-graph (following
outgoing edges) around every mapped concept. Token selection
(Section~\ref{sec:method:selection}) only ever considers $t \in V_D$: a
candidate that no mapped concept can reach within $D$ hops has zero
possible contribution to the objective below and is never evaluated.

$D$ trades off computational cost against coverage: a larger $D$ grows the
candidate pool, deepens context trees, and widens the coverage horizon, at
proportionally higher cost, while a smaller $D$ is cheaper but risks
excluding concepts that only a broader candidate could represent. The
value used in our experiments, and how we chose it, is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results:candidates}.

\subsection{Semantic coverage score}
\label{sec:method:score}
\begin{quote}\small\itshape
\begin{itemize}
\item Greedy token selection based on semantic coverage with lambda as distance penality (add illustration)
\end{itemize}
\end{quote}
Given the candidate pool $V_D$, we first need a way to measure how well a
set of tokens represents a concept, before we can ask which set of $k$
tokens does so best. For $u \in V$, $T \subseteq V$, and horizon
$h \in \{0, \dots, D\}$, let $S_h(u, T) \in [0, 1]$ denote how well $u$ is
covered by $T$ when at most $h$ further relationships may be traversed. The
base case is
\[
S_0(u, T) = \mathbf{1}[u \in T],
\]
and, extending the recursion with a per-hop distance penalty $\lambda \in
(0, 1]$,
\begin{equation}
S_h(u, T) =
\begin{cases}
1, & u \in T, \\[2pt]
0, & u \notin T \text{ and } d^+(u) = 0, \\[2pt]
\dfrac{\lambda}{d^+(u)} \displaystyle\sum_{(u,r,v) \in \mathrm{Out}(u)} S_{h-1}(v, T), & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:coverage}
\end{equation}
$d^+(u)$ denotes the out-degree of $u$. In words: a token is absorbing (its coverage is $1$ at every remaining
horizon), and every other node's coverage is the $\lambda$-discounted
average coverage of its out-neighbors. Setting $\lambda = 1$ recovers the
undiscounted score, in which a token found $j$ hops away and a token found
at the root contribute equally; for $\lambda < 1$, a token found $j$ hops
away instead contributes $\lambda^j$, so the score also rewards
\emph{proximity} to a token, not only reachability. The overall objective
is the mean coverage of the mapped concepts at the full horizon $D$:
\[
F_D(T) = \frac{1}{|M|} \sum_{c \in M} S_D(c, T), \qquad F_D(\varnothing) = 0,\ \ 0 \le F_D(T) \le 1.
\]

This objective has a property that makes it tractable at scale: $F_D$ is
normalized, monotone, and submodular in $T$ (the marginal gain of adding a
token never increases as more tokens are already selected), which follows
from writing $F_D$ as a positive average, over random depth-$D$ walks from
each $c \in M$, of the indicator that the walk hits $T$. Section~\ref{sec:method:selection}
builds directly on this property to select tokens efficiently and with a
guarantee on solution quality.

\subsection{Greedy marginal-gain token selection}
\label{sec:method:selection}
Given the coverage score $F_D$ (Section~\ref{sec:method:score}), we now
need a rule for choosing which $k$ members of $V_D$ become tokens. For
$T \subseteq V_D$ and a candidate $t \in V_D \setminus T$, the
\emph{marginal gain} of adding $t$ to $T$ is
\[
\Delta(t \mid T) := F_D(T \cup \{t\}) - F_D(T).
\]
Greedy selection builds $T$ one token at a time, at each step adding the
candidate with the largest marginal gain given what has already been
selected. Submodularity of $F_D$ buys two things. First, the classical
greedy approximation guarantee,
\begin{equation}
F_D(T^{\text{greedy}}_k) \ge \Big(1 - \tfrac{1}{e}\Big) \max_{|T| \le k} F_D(T).
\label{eq:guarantee}
\end{equation}
Second, and more important for a candidate pool of tens of thousands of
nodes, it licenses \emph{lazy} greedy: each candidate's previously computed
marginal gain is a valid upper bound on its current gain, so at every step
it suffices to re-verify only the best stale candidate against the
next-best stale bound, instead of recomputing every remaining candidate's
gain from scratch \citeneeded{minoux1978accelerated}.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:select-tokens} states this loop precisely; it calls
\textsc{CandidateDelta} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:candidate-delta}), which
computes one candidate's exact marginal gain $\Delta(t \mid T)$ by
propagating a sparse \emph{delta} backward through the incoming-edge
structure -- adding a candidate $t$ only ever changes $S_h$ at $t$ itself
and at vertices that can reach $t$ within $D$ hops in reverse -- instead of
recomputing the full $O(D(|V|+|E|))$ recursion of Eq.~\eqref{eq:coverage}
from scratch for every candidate at every step.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{\textsc{SelectTokens} -- lazy-greedy token selection}
\label{alg:select-tokens}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require candidate pool $V_D$, mapped concepts $M$, horizon $D$, budget $k$, penalty $\lambda$
\Ensure token set $T$ with $|T| = k$ (or fewer if candidates run out)
\State $T \gets \varnothing$;\ $S \gets \mathbf{0}$ \Comment{$S_h(u,T){=}0$ for all $u,h$ when $T=\varnothing$}
\ForAll{$t \in V_D$} \Comment{initial seeding pass -- exact gains, parallelizable}
    \State $(\text{gain}, \_) \gets \Call{CandidateDelta}{t}$
    \State push $(t, \text{gain})$ onto max-heap $H$
\EndFor
\While{$|T| < k$ and $H \neq \varnothing$}
    \Repeat
        \State $t \gets$ pop top of $H$
        \State $(\text{gain}, \delta) \gets \Call{CandidateDelta}{t}$ \Comment{re-verify exactly, not from a stale bound}
        \State $b \gets$ gain bound now at the top of $H$ (or $-\infty$ if empty)
    \Until{$\text{gain} \ge b$} \Comment{submodularity: a stale bound never overstates a candidate's true gain}
    \State $T \gets T \cup \{t\}$;\ \Call{Commit}{$t, \delta$}
\EndWhile
\State \Return $T$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{\textsc{CandidateDelta} and \textsc{Commit} -- sparse incremental update}
\label{alg:candidate-delta}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{CandidateDelta}{$t$}
    \State $\delta_0 \gets \{\, t \mapsto 1 - S_0(t) \,\}$ \Comment{only $t$ changes at horizon $0$}
    \For{$h = 1, \dots, D$}
        \State $\delta_h \gets \{\}$
        \ForAll{$(v, d_v) \in \delta_{h-1}$}
            \ForAll{$u \in \mathrm{In}(v)$ with $u \notin T \cup \{t\}$}
                \State $\delta_h[u] \gets \delta_h[u] + \lambda\, d_v / d^+(u)$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:coverage}, propagated backward}
            \EndFor
        \EndFor
        \State $\delta_h[t] \gets 1 - S_h(t)$ \Comment{$t$ is absorbing at every horizon}
    \EndFor
    \State $\text{gain} \gets \frac{1}{|M|}\sum_{c \in M} \delta_D[c]$
    \State \Return $(\text{gain}, \delta)$
\EndFunction
\Function{Commit}{$t$, $\delta$}
    \For{$h = 0, \dots, D$}
        \ForAll{$(u, d_u) \in \delta_h$}
            \State $S_h(u) \gets S_h(u) + d_u$
        \EndFor
    \EndFor
    \State $T \gets T \cup \{t\}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Complexity.} Let $R_D(t) = \{u \in V_D : t \text{ is reachable
from } u \text{ within } D \text{ hops}\}$ be the reverse $D$-hop
neighborhood of $t$: the only vertices whose coverage
\textsc{CandidateDelta} can change. One call to \textsc{CandidateDelta}$(t)$
costs $O(D \cdot |E(R_D(t))|)$, $D$ rounds of backward propagation, each
touching the edges among vertices in $R_D(t)$ at most once, which is far
cheaper than recomputing Eq.~\eqref{eq:coverage} for the whole pool,
$O(D(|V_D|+|E_D|))$, whenever $R_D(t)$ is a small local neighborhood rather
than all of $V_D$, the common case in a sparse graph like SNOMED~CT.

The initial seeding pass makes $|V_D|$ such calls in parallel,
then builds the max-heap in $O(|V_D| \log |V_D|)$. Submodularity guarantees
a stale gain is always an upper bound on a candidate's true current gain
\citeneeded{minoux1978accelerated}, so re-verifying the heap's top
candidate against the next-best stale bound never lets a suboptimal
candidate through. In the worst case every candidate still needs
re-verifying at every step, giving the same $O(k \cdot |V_D|)$
\textsc{CandidateDelta} calls as naive greedy; in practice, consistent with
lazy greedy's known behavior on submodular objectives, only a small,
roughly constant number of candidates need re-verification per accepted
token, since diminishing marginal gains make a stale loser unlikely to
overtake the current leader later. Selecting $k$ tokens therefore costs
$O(k \log |V_D|)$ heap operations plus between $O(k)$ and $O(k \cdot
|V_D|)$ \textsc{CandidateDelta} calls, on top of the one-time,
parallelizable $O(|V_D|)$ seeding pass.



\subsection{Tokenization into context-tree representation}
\label{sec:method:tokenizer}

The lazy-greedy procedure in \S\ref{sec:method:selection} produces the token vocabulary $T$. Selecting $T$ answers ``which concepts become tokens.'' A separate question is how a mapped concept $c \in M$ should be represented once $T$ is fixed. The simplest option -- the flat, unordered set of tokens reachable from $c$ -- throws away exactly the information that non-\texttt{IS\_A} relationships were designed to carry: it discards \emph{which} attribute value pairs with \emph{which}, collapsing distinct clinical configurations into the same bag of tokens. SNOMED CT concept 86299006 (Tetralogy of Fallot) illustrates why this matters. Its defining relationships include four morphology--site pairs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Hypertrophy} -- \emph{right ventricular structure}
    \item \emph{Stenosis} -- \emph{pulmonary valve structure}
    \item \emph{Overriding structure} -- \emph{aorta}
    \item \emph{Septal defect} -- \emph{interventricular septum structure}
\end{itemize}

If we flatten these into an unordered bag of tokens -- \{Hypertrophy, Stenosis, Overriding structure, Septal defect, right ventricular structure, pulmonary valve structure, aorta, interventricular septum structure\} -- nothing in the representation says which morphology attaches to which site. A model given only this bag cannot distinguish the actual concept from a hypothetical, clinically distinct configuration where, say, the septal defect involves the pulmonary valve structure instead of the interventricular septum. Relationship groups exist precisely to rule out this kind of cross-pairing, and a tokenizer that discards them reintroduces the ambiguity SNOMED CT was designed to avoid.

This is a general problem, not one specific to congenital heart disease: any concept whose definition pairs two or more attribute values of the same relationship type -- most visibly laterality, where a finding is present on one paired structure and absent, or different, on its contralateral counterpart -- loses that pairing under flattening, and with it the distinction between two clinically different presentations.

We preserve this information with a \emph{context tree}. Starting from a mapped concept $c$, the tree mirrors the traversal used to build the candidate pool (\S\ref{sec:method:candidates}), but with one distinction: \texttt{IS\_A} edges are \emph{transparent} and continue the search within the current context, while every other relationship type opens a fresh \emph{subcontext}, keyed by the pair $(r, v)$ of relationship type and destination concept. Two independent occurrences of the same non-\texttt{IS\_A} relationship type within the same context are therefore kept apart as distinct subcontexts, rather than collapsed into the same bag.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:expand} formalizes this. It is invoked once per mapped concept as \textsc{Expand}$(c, q_0, 0)$, where $q_0$ is a fresh root context. The resulting tree keeps only $T$ as its vocabulary; its subcontext structure records exactly which relationship chain each token was reached through. This is what preserves the pairing above -- e.g., that \emph{Hypertrophy} attaches to the \emph{right ventricular structure} and not to the \emph{pulmonary valve structure}, and that \emph{Overriding structure} attaches to the \emph{aorta} -- distinctions a flat token set would collapse into an unordered bag.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Expand}$(u, q, d)$}
\label{alg:expand}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Procedure{Expand}{$u, q, d$}
    \If{$u \in T$}
        \State attach $u$ as a token of context $q$ at depth $d$
        \State \Return
    \EndIf
    \If{$d = D$ \textbf{or} $u$ has no outgoing edges}
        \State mark $q$ uncovered on this branch
        \State \Return
    \EndIf
    \For{each outgoing edge $(u, r, v)$, non-\texttt{IS\_A} edges first}
        \If{$r = \texttt{IS\_A}$}
            \State \Call{Expand}{$v, q, d+1$} \Comment{transparent: same context}
        \Else
            \If{$(r, v)$ is new for $q$}
                \State create subcontext $q'$ of $q$ keyed by $(r, v)$
                \State \Call{Expand}{$v, q', d+1$}
            \EndIf
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:method:metrics}

\begin{quote}\small\itshape

\item 3.1: Efficientcy: conciseness, tree\_complexity
\item 3.2: distance\_score: Fidelity
\item 3.3: discrimitive power: uniqueness\_entropy, unique\_rate
\item 3.4: semantic content: semantic\_coverage, uncovered\_rate, unk\_branche\_rate
\item and also say either they are hogher or lower better (interpretation)
\end{itemize}

With a fixed $T$ and context trees for all of $M$ in hand, we can now ask
how good this representation actually is. All eight metrics below are
computed from the tokenized concepts, as opposed to $F_D(T)$,

(Section~\ref{sec:method:selection}). Table~\ref{tab:metrics} groups them

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:method:baselines}

\begin{quote}\small\itshape
\begin{itemize}
\item Chosen baseline for token selection (all DAG compatible)
\item 4.0: k random
\item 4.1: degree based: Most children, Highest degree, Highest degree with D <= 1
\item 4.2: centrality based: Closeness centrality, Page rank, Personalized page rank
\item 4.3 ours, and also our ablated version: Discrete set cover
\end{itemize}
\end{quote}

We compare our method against seven baselines from three families, plus one
ablation of our own method, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:baselines}. All of
them are chosen to be well defined on a DAG. Every baseline ranks the full
candidate pool $V_D$ once and is then truncated to the top-$k$ prefix of
that ranking, exactly as our own method's selection history is truncated --
so every method in Section~\ref{sec:results} is compared at matched $k$.

The discrete set-cover ablation is the one entry that is not a baseline in
the usual sense: it isolates what the soft, $\lambda$-decayed, multi-hop
coverage of Eq.~\eqref{eq:coverage} buys over textbook hard-coverage greedy,
using the \emph{same} selection mechanism, so any difference in
Section~\ref{sec:results} can be attributed to the coverage formulation
itself rather than to the greedy procedure around it.
