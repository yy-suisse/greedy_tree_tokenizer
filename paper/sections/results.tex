Three things hold across the entire tested range ($k \in [500, 19{,}500]$,
i.e.\ up to roughly half of $|M| \approx 40{,}000$), not just at the k-values
tabulated above. First, every structured method -- ours, both degree-based,
and all three centrality-based baselines -- reaches semantic coverage
$\ge 0.999$ within the tested range, while \emph{$k$-random} does not: it
only reaches $0.804$ at the largest $k$ tested, still well short of every
structured method. Second, our own method's envelope (best over
$\lambda \in \{0.2,\dots,1.0\}$) is the fastest of all five families to reach
$\ge 0.999$ coverage, doing so already at $k=500$, the smallest budget
tested -- ahead of centrality ($k=1{,}000$), degree ($k=3{,}000$), the
discrete-set-cover ablation (never reaches $0.999$ within the tested range,
topping out at $0.999$ only at $k=19{,}500$), and random (never reaches it).
Third, $k$-random's gap to every structured method does not close within the
tested range: it is still roughly $0.2$ below the nearest structured method
at $k=19{,}500$.

\subsection{Comparison across baseline families}
\label{sec:results:families}

\begin{quote}\small\itshape
\begin{itemize}
\item analysis of metrics among different families of candidate selection, and the characteristics:
\item degree based: may select semantic overlapped concept, less semantic preservation, redundant selection in same branch(?)
\item centrality based: random walk based, less semantic preservation (?), overlapped semantics(?), maybe less efficiency (?)
\item ours
\item in this part, for each family, i will plot the upper envelop curve of metric performance
\end{itemize}
\end{quote}

\textbf{Degree-based.} Within this family, \emph{highest degree} (all
relation types) dominates \emph{most children} (\texttt{IS\_A} only) on
every metric we checked (distance score, conciseness, uniqueness entropy,
unique rate) at every $k$ we sampled, with one narrow exception: between
$k \approx 2{,}500$ and $k \approx 4{,}500$, \emph{most children} briefly has
very slightly \emph{higher} semantic coverage (e.g.\ $0.999$ vs.\ $0.996$ at
$k=3{,}000$) before \emph{highest degree} overtakes it again by $k=5{,}000$
and stays ahead for the rest of the range. \notetoself{this local crossover
is real, not a plotting artifact -- verified directly against the parquet
rows -- but it's narrow enough that I would state the headline claim as
"highest degree dominates most children almost everywhere" rather than
"always," and mention the crossover as a footnote rather than lead with it.
The pattern you predicted qualitatively (non-hierarchy relations carrying
extra coverage signal a pure-\texttt{IS\_A} heuristic misses) does hold as
the overall story -- just not exceptionlessly.}

\textbf{Centrality-based.} \emph{Personalized PageRank}, the one
$M$-aware member of this family, reaches near-ceiling coverage fastest within
the family ($k=1{,}000$), ahead of plain PageRank and closeness centrality
-- consistent with the idea that restarting on $M$ rather than uniformly
should help when the objective is itself about covering $M$.

\textbf{Ours vs. every baseline family, on coverage speed.} At small $k$
(500--1{,}000, i.e.\ roughly 1--2.5\% of $|M|$), our $\lambda$-sweep envelope
is the only family already at or above $0.998$ semantic coverage; every
baseline family needs a larger $k$ to catch up (Table~\ref{tab:coverage-trend}).

\textbf{Discriminative power (uniqueness entropy / unique rate).}
\doubt{your hypothesis that degree-based methods would show \emph{lower}
discriminative power than ours, from selecting semantically-overlapping
candidates, is only partly borne out: at $k=1{,}000$, the best degree-based
member reaches unique rate $0.588$, close to our envelope's $0.594$ -- not
the large gap the hypothesis predicted. Where a real gap does show up is at
$k=15{,}000$: centrality-based baselines slightly \emph{overtake} our
envelope on both uniqueness entropy ($0.989$ vs.\ $0.987$) and unique rate
($0.903$ vs.\ $0.893$) -- the one place in this sweep where a baseline family
is not dominated by ours. Worth stating honestly rather than glossing over;
it doesn't undermine the coverage-speed story above, but it means "ours wins
on every metric at every $k$" would not be an accurate claim.}

\textbf{Ablation: soft vs. hard coverage.} The discrete set-cover baseline
(Section~\ref{sec:method:baselines}) -- same lazy/CELF greedy mechanism and
approximation guarantee as our method, but a binary rather than
$\lambda$-decayed coverage signal -- is the slowest of all \emph{structured}
methods to approach ceiling coverage: it never reaches $\ge 0.999$ within
the tested range (topping out at $0.999$ exactly at $k=19{,}500$), behind
every degree-based and centrality-based baseline as well as our own method.
This is a direct empirical confirmation that the soft, distance-aware
formulation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:coverage} buys something concrete over
classical hard-coverage greedy, not just a theoretical nicety.

Checking every one of the 39 tested $k$ values (not just the $k=10{,}000$
snapshot in Table~\ref{tab:lambda-sweep}), three distinct patterns emerge,
not two:

\textbf{Monotonically improving toward $\lambda=1$ (100\% of $k$ values).}
Semantic coverage and unk rate improve monotonically as $\lambda$ increases
from $0.2$ to $1.0$, at \emph{every single} tested $k$, always best at
$\lambda=1.0$. Uncovered rate follows the same direction but saturates to
exactly $0$ well before $\lambda=1.0$ is reached: by $\lambda \ge 0.6$ it is
already $0$ at every $k \ge 1{,}500$, and even $\lambda=0.2$ reaches $0$ by
$k=5{,}000$.

\textbf{Peaked at $\lambda \approx 0.8$ (large majority of $k$ values).}
Distance score is maximized at $\lambda=0.8$ at \emph{all 39} tested $k$
values, dropping at $\lambda=1.0$ every time (e.g.\ $0.863 \to 0.728$ at
$k=10{,}000$). Tree complexity is minimized at $\lambda=0.8$ at 38 of 39 $k$
values; conciseness at 34 of 39; uniqueness entropy at 30 of 39; unique rate
at 27 of 39 (with $\lambda=0.6$ the runner-up choice in most of the
remaining cases). This is the confirmed version of your outline's
prediction -- these metrics really do trade off against raw coverage, and
$\lambda=0.8$ is consistently, not just occasionally, the best single
setting for this group.

\textbf{Its own pattern: exact rate.} Exact rate does not fit either group
above. Among the positive $\lambda$ values, it is highest at
$\lambda=0.2$ at \emph{every} tested $k$ and decreases overall as $\lambda$
increases toward $1$ -- moving in the \emph{opposite} direction from
semantic coverage and unk rate as $\lambda$ increases. It is not perfectly
monotonic on the way down (e.g.\ at $k=10{,}000$, $\lambda=1.0$'s $0.185$ is
a partial rebound above $\lambda=0.8$'s $0.155$), but $\lambda=0.2$ is the
single best positive-$\lambda$ choice for this metric without exception in
the sweep.

\textbf{The degenerate case $\lambda=0$: proven, and confirmed empirically.}
As derived in Eq.~\eqref{eq:coverage}: at $\lambda=0$, every "otherwise"
term vanishes, so $S_h(u,T) = S_0(u,T) = \mathbf{1}[u \in T]$ at
\emph{every} horizon, and $F_D(T)|_{\lambda=0}$ reduces exactly to
$\text{exact\_rate}(M,T)$ -- selecting at $\lambda=0$ optimizes a
fundamentally different, narrower objective than coverage. This is not just
an algebraic curiosity: it is exactly what the sweep shows. Across all 39
$k$ values, the $\lambda=0$ selection has (a) \emph{strictly the highest}
exact rate of every $\lambda \in \{0, 0.2, \dots, 1.0\}$ at every single $k$
(e.g.\ $0.217$ at $k=10{,}000$, vs.\ $0.199$ for the next best, $\lambda=0.2$)
-- exactly as predicted, since it is directly what $\lambda=0$ selection
optimizes -- and (b) \emph{strictly the lowest} semantic coverage of every
$\lambda$ at every single $k$, dramatically so at small $k$: $0.012$ at
$k=500$, actually \emph{below} $k$-random's $0.050$ at the same budget, and
still only $0.491$ at the largest tested $k=19{,}500$ (vs.\ $\ge 0.999$ for
every $\lambda \ge 0.2$). \notetoself{note for precision: the
\emph{selection}-time objective at $\lambda=0$ literally equals
$\text{exact\_rate}$, but the reported \texttt{semantic\_coverage} column is
always \emph{evaluated} at $\lambda=1$ (Section~\ref{sec:method:metrics}'s
convention) -- so the two numbers in the table are not the same quantity
computed twice; the point is that a token set optimized for exact\_rate
performs catastrophically under the standard $\lambda=1$ coverage evaluation
everyone else is judged by.}

\textbf{Practical takeaway.} Given the above, $\lambda \approx 0.8$ is a
reasonable default when concise, nearby representations matter as much as
raw coverage: it costs essentially nothing in semantic coverage or unk rate
relative to $\lambda=1$ (both already at or near ceiling by moderate $k$),
while giving the best distance score, tree complexity, and (usually)
conciseness and discriminative power in the whole sweep. $\lambda=1$ remains
the right choice if raw coverage at the smallest possible $k$ is the only
thing that matters, or if a downstream use case specifically wants the
highest possible exact-match rate among large-$\lambda$ settings.
