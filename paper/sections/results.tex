We use every SNOMED~CT concept mapped by our institution's semantician team
in our local hospital categorical data  as the mapped set $M$, resulting 46,150 distinct SNOMED CT concepts.

\subsection{Candidate pool subgraph}
\label{sec:results:candidates}

Candidate tokens are drawn from $G_D$, the subgraph reached by
following outgoing edges out of $M$ up to depth $D$
(\S\ref{sec:method:candidates}). We set $D = 9$ for computational
reasons: tokenizing against the full, unbounded horizon would make
the candidate pool, and every downstream step built on it,
considerably slower to construct. This choice costs little coverage
in practice: $D=9$ already reaches 98.4\% of the concepts reachable
from $M$ at any depth (Table~\ref{tab:snomed-stats}). Restricting to
$D=9$ narrows the pool to $|V_D| = 75{,}964$ concepts and $|E_D| =
293{,}146$ edges, forming a subgraph $G_D$ with 2 weakly connected
components. All methods compared below select their tokens from
this same pool, so any difference between them is a difference in
\emph{which} tokens are chosen, not in what they had access to.

\paragraph{Precision and interpretability.} Semantic coverage behaves
as before: every structured method reaches $\ge 0.999$ somewhere in the
tested range, $k$-random does not (topping out at $0.804$ even at
$k=19{,}500$, $\rho=42.3\%$), and $\lambda=1.0$ reaches it fastest, already
at $k=500$ ($\rho=1.1\%$). Distance score and unique rate tell a
different story: $\lambda=1.0$ is consistently one of the \emph{weakest}
structured methods on both, and the gap to the best baseline does not
close as $k$ grows -- on unique rate, its gap to personalized PageRank
widens to roughly $-0.29$ by $k=12{,}500$ ($\rho=27.1\%$) and stays
there through $k=19{,}500$. More strikingly, $\lambda=1.0$'s unique rate
is overtaken by \emph{$k$-random} at $k=12{,}000$ ($\rho=26.0\%$) and
stays behind it for the rest of the tested range, despite $k$-random
still trailing badly on every other metric -- a direct consequence of
optimizing purely for coverage. $\lambda=0.8$ does not share this
weakness: it leads every structured method on distance score and unique
rate at every tested $k$, while still reaching $\ge 0.999$ semantic
coverage at $k=1{,}000$ ($\rho=2.2\%$) -- one step behind $\lambda=1.0$.
Unknown rate favors $\lambda=1.0$ outright: it is the first method to
reach $0$ unknown rate, already by $k=1{,}000$ ($\rho=2.2\%$), while
personalized PageRank still has a $9.9\%$ unknown rate at that budget
and does not reach $0$ within the tested range. $k$-random remains far
behind on semantic coverage, distance score, and unknown rate across the
full range, still at a $63.4\%$ unknown rate and a distance score of
just $0.605$ at $k=19{,}500$ ($\rho=42.3\%$).

\paragraph{Computing efficiency.} Tree complexity falls monotonically
with $k$ for every method, as expected -- a larger vocabulary lets more
concepts resolve to shorter context trees. The ordering among methods
mirrors the precision metrics: $\lambda=0.8$ produces the smallest trees
at every $k$ from $5{,}000$ onward ($\rho \ge 10.8\%$), reaching $3.39$
at $k=19{,}500$ ($\rho=42.3\%$), while $\lambda=1.0$ is again mid-pack,
larger than personalized PageRank and highest degree throughout the
range (e.g.\ $6.21$ vs.\ $4.88$ at $k=10{,}000$, $\rho=21.7\%$).

Conciseness, the average number of tokens used to represent a
concept, tells a closely related story for the five structured
methods: it falls sharply with $k$, from the low-to-mid 30s and 40s at
$k=500$ ($\rho=1.1\%$) down to single digits by $k=19{,}500$
($\rho=42.3\%$), and the ranking again favors $\lambda=0.8$ (4.13, the
lowest of all structured methods at $k=19{,}500$) over $\lambda=1.0$
(6.99, mid-pack, similar to its position on tree complexity). $k$-random
is the exception, and not in a good way: its conciseness \emph{rises}
from $1.76$ at $k=500$ to a peak near $16$ around $k=14{,}000$
($\rho=30.3\%$) before drifting back down to $12.21$ at $k=19{,}500$ --
the opposite direction from every structured method. This is an
artifact of its uncovered rate, not a genuine efficiency advantage:
$k$-random leaves $71.2\%$ of concepts completely uncovered at
$k=500$, so its small-$k$ conciseness average is taken over only the
few concepts that happen to land a trivial one-token match by chance;
as $k$ grows and uncovered rate falls toward $1.5\%$ by $k=19{,}500$,
previously-excluded, harder-to-cover concepts enter the average and
pull it up. $k$-random's conciseness is therefore not comparable to the
structured methods' at small $k$, and should be read alongside its
uncovered rate rather than on its own.

Discrete set-cover and $k$-random produce the largest, least efficient
trees at every budget tested on tree complexity, and discrete set-cover
is consistently the least concise of the five structured methods as
well, reinforcing that the ablation underperforms on efficiency, not
only on coverage.

Across all five metrics, the same pattern holds: $\lambda=1.0$ wins
decisively on semantic coverage and unknown rate -- the two metrics
measuring whether a concept is covered at all -- but gives up ground on
distance score, unique rate, and tree complexity, which measure the
quality and efficiency of the representation once a concept \emph{is}
covered, to the point of losing to $k$-random on unique rate at large
$k$. $\lambda=0.8$ trades a small, one-step delay in reaching full
coverage ($k=1{,}000$ vs.\ $k=500$, $\rho=2.2\%$ vs.\ $1.1\%$) for
representations that are more distance-faithful, more unique, and more
concise across nearly the entire tested range.

Across all six metrics, ours holds the envelope outright on semantic coverage, distance score, and uncovered rate for every $k$ we test, and is competitive-to-best on unique rate, conciseness, and tree complexity, trading the lead with degree-based at small $k$ ($k\le 10{,}000$, $\rho \le 21.7\%$) and with centrality-based on unique rate beyond the $k=10{,}500$ crossover ($\rho=22.8\%$). \texttt{discrete\_set\_cover} is dominated on every metric except uncovered rate, where it ties the other families at $0$ from $k=5{,}000$ ($\rho=10.8\%$) onward — consistent with its binary coverage objective reaching every concept without selecting the sharpest token once covered.

\subsection{Effect of the distance penalty \texorpdfstring{$\lambda$}{lambda}}
\label{sec:results:lambda}

Checking every one of the 39 tested $k$ values, not just the $k=10{,}000$
snapshot in Table~\ref{tab:lambda-sweep}, three distinct patterns emerge:

\textbf{Monotonically improving toward $\lambda=1$ (100\% of $k$ values).}
Semantic coverage and unk rate improve monotonically as $\lambda$ increases
from $0.1$ to $1.0$, at every single tested $k$, always best at
$\lambda=1.0$. Uncovered rate follows the same direction but saturates to
exactly $0$ well before $\lambda=1.0$, and the threshold is itself
$\lambda$-dependent: $\lambda=0.9$ reaches $0$ by $k=1{,}000$ and
$\lambda\ge0.7$ by $k=1{,}500$; the mid-range values follow in sequence
($\lambda=0.6$ by $k=3{,}500$, $\lambda=0.5$ by $k=4{,}000$,
$\lambda\in\{0.3,0.4\}$ by $k=4{,}500$), and even $\lambda=0.1$--$0.2$ reach
$0$ by $k=5{,}000$.

\textbf{Peaked at $\lambda \approx 0.9$ (large majority of $k$ values).} In
the other direction, distance score is maximized at $\lambda=0.9$ at
\emph{all} 39 tested $k$ values, dropping again at $\lambda=1.0$ every time
(e.g.\ $0.865 \to 0.728$ at $k=10{,}000$). The same peak shows up, slightly
less consistently, in two other metrics: tree complexity is minimized at
$\lambda=0.9$ at 38 of 39 $k$ values (the lone exception, $k=500$, is won by
$\lambda=0.6$), and conciseness -- restricting to $\lambda>0$ for the reason
noted under Table~\ref{tab:lambda-sweep} -- at 34 of 39. Unique rate shows
the same direction but more weakly: $\lambda=0.9$ is best at 21 of 39 $k$
values, a plurality rather than a majority, with $\lambda=0.5$ the most
frequent runner-up (5 of 39). This confirms your outline's prediction that
these metrics trade off against raw coverage, and shows that $\lambda=0.9$
-- not the coarser $\lambda=0.8$ a half-decile grid might suggest -- is the
single best setting for this group.

\textbf{Its own pattern: exact rate.} Exact rate follows neither group
above. Among the positive $\lambda$ values, it is highest at $\lambda=0.1$
at every tested $k$, decreasing monotonically (zero violations across all
39 $k$) as $\lambda$ increases from $0.1$ to $0.9$ -- moving \emph{opposite}
to semantic coverage and unk rate. Only the final step breaks the pattern:
at $\lambda=1.0$ exact rate partially rebounds above $\lambda=0.9$ at 33 of
the 39 tested $k$ values (e.g.\ at $k=10{,}000$, $0.185$ vs.\ $0.149$).
Still, $\lambda=0.1$ is, without exception, the best positive-$\lambda$
choice for this metric.

\textbf{The degenerate case $\lambda=0$.} Setting $\lambda=0$ is not merely
a bad setting; Eq.~\eqref{eq:coverage} shows it is a qualitatively different
objective. At $\lambda=0$, every "otherwise" term vanishes, so
$S_h(u,T) = S_0(u,T) = \mathbf{1}[u \in T]$ at every horizon, and
$F_D(T)|_{\lambda=0}$ reduces exactly to $\text{exact\_rate}(M,T)$: selecting
at $\lambda=0$ optimizes exact-match rate, not coverage. The sweep confirms
this is not just an algebraic curiosity. Across all 39 $k$ values, the
$\lambda=0$ selection has \emph{strictly the highest} exact rate of every
tested $\lambda$ at every single $k$ (e.g.\ $0.217$ at $k=10{,}000$, vs.\
$0.208$ for the next best, $\lambda=0.1$) -- exactly as predicted, since
this is directly what $\lambda=0$ optimizes -- and, at the same time,
\emph{strictly the lowest} semantic coverage of every $\lambda$ at every
single $k$, dramatically so at small $k$: $0.012$ at $k=500$, actually
\emph{below} $k$-random's $0.050$ at the same budget, and still only
$0.491$ at the largest tested $k=19{,}500$ (vs.\ $0.995$--$1.000$ for every
$\lambda \ge 0.1$, crossing $0.999$ from $\lambda\ge0.3$ onward).
\notetoself{note for precision: the \emph{selection}-time objective at
$\lambda=0$ literally equals exact\_rate, but the reported
\texttt{semantic\_coverage} column is always \emph{evaluated} at
$\lambda=1$ (Section~\ref{sec:method:metrics}'s convention), so the two
numbers are not the same quantity computed twice -- the point is that a
token set optimized for exact\_rate performs catastrophically under the
standard $\lambda=1$ coverage evaluation everyone else is judged by. Same
caution applies to $\lambda=0$'s conciseness: it is not a genuine efficiency
result, for the identical reason $k$-random's small-$k$ conciseness wasn't
-- both are averages computed almost entirely over trivially-covered
concepts.}

\textbf{Practical reading.} No single $\lambda$ dominates every metric, so
the right choice is a genuine trade-off rather than a free lunch: $\lambda=1$
for applications that need maximal, unambiguous concept coverage;
$\lambda\approx0.9$ when distance score, tree complexity, or conciseness
matter more than the last fraction of a percent of coverage;
$\lambda\approx0.1$ if exact lexical fidelity to the source concept is the
priority and some coverage loss is acceptable; and $\lambda=0$ only as a
limit case, useful for isolating exact-match behavior but not a deployable
operating point on its own. We treat $\lambda\in[0.9,1.0]$ as our default
recommended range and report both endpoints throughout; a practitioner with
different priorities may reasonably prefer a different point on this
curve. Token-level reuse rate -- how often the same token is reused across
distinct concepts within a selection -- is a natural complement to this
picture but is not yet implemented in our evaluation pipeline; we leave it
to future work.

\textbf{Practical takeaway.} Putting the four patterns together,
$\lambda \approx 0.8$ is a reasonable default when concise, nearby
representations matter as much as raw coverage: it costs essentially
nothing in semantic coverage or unk rate relative to $\lambda=1$ (both
already at or near ceiling by moderate $k$), while giving the best distance
score, tree complexity, and usually conciseness and discriminative power in
the whole sweep. $\lambda=1$ remains the right choice if raw coverage at the
smallest possible $k$ is the only thing that matters, or if a downstream use
case specifically wants the highest possible exact-match rate among
large-$\lambda$ settings. \doubt{"depends on personal choice" -- happy to
