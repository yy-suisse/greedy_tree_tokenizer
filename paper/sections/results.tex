\subsection{Candidate pool}
\label{sec:results:candidates}

\begin{quote}\small\itshape
\begin{itemize}
\item how to get subgraph and narrow down the candidate pool, then give the number 400k -> 70k
\end{itemize}
\end{quote}

Before comparing methods, we first need to know how large the candidate
pool actually is. Candidate tokens are restricted to the $D$-hop
neighborhood of $M$ (Section~\ref{sec:method:candidates}), which narrows the
pool from $|V| \approx 400{,}000$ SNOMED concepts to \textbf{75{,}964}
concepts under the current setting ($D=9$), spread across \textbf{2} weakly
connected components (Table~\ref{tab:snomed-stats}). \notetoself{also noted
in Section~\ref{sec:background}: this is barely larger than the old $D{=}3$
figure ($\sim$75{,}634) despite the much larger depth budget -- the
ego-graph around $M$ saturates quickly.} All methods compared below select
their tokens from this same 75{,}964-concept pool, so any difference between
them is a difference in \emph{which} tokens are chosen, not in what they had
access to.

Three findings hold across the entire tested range ($k \in [500, 19{,}500]$,
i.e.\ up to roughly half of $|M| \approx 40{,}000$), not just at the
$k$-values tabulated above. First, every \emph{structured} method -- ours,
both degree-based, and all three centrality-based baselines -- reaches
semantic coverage $\ge 0.999$ somewhere in the tested range, while
\emph{$k$-random} does not: it only reaches $0.804$ at the largest $k$
tested, still well short of every structured method. Second, our own
method's envelope (best over $\lambda \in \{0.2,\dots,1.0\}$) is the fastest
of all five families to reach $\ge 0.999$ coverage, doing so already at
$k=500$, the smallest budget tested -- ahead of centrality ($k=1{,}000$),
degree ($k=3{,}000$), the discrete-set-cover ablation (never reaches
$0.999$ within the tested range, topping out at exactly $0.999$ only at
$k=19{,}500$), and random (never reaches it). Third, $k$-random's gap to
every structured method does not close within the tested range: it is still
roughly $0.2$ below the nearest structured method even at $k=19{,}500$.

The overall speed ranking above raises a follow-up question: does it hold
\emph{within} each family too, and does it hold on every metric, not just
semantic coverage? We check each family in turn, then compare all of them
against ours directly.

\textbf{Degree-based.} Within this family, \emph{highest degree} (all
relation types) dominates \emph{most children} (\texttt{IS\_A} only) on
distance score, conciseness, uniqueness entropy, and unique rate at every
$k$ we sampled. Semantic coverage is the one exception, and it is narrow
enough to deserve its own table rather than a parenthetical number:
Table~\ref{tab:degree-crossover} shows that between $k=2{,}500$ and
$k=4{,}500$, \emph{most children} briefly has \emph{higher} semantic
coverage than \emph{highest degree}, before \emph{highest degree} overtakes
it again at $k=5{,}000$ and stays ahead for the rest of the tested range.

\textbf{Centrality-based.} \emph{Personalized PageRank}, the one $M$-aware
member of this family, reaches near-ceiling coverage fastest within the
family ($k=1{,}000$), ahead of plain PageRank and closeness centrality --
consistent with the idea that restarting on $M$ rather than uniformly should
help when the objective is itself about covering $M$.

\textbf{Ours vs.\ every baseline family, on coverage speed.} At small $k$

is the only family already at or above $0.998$ semantic coverage; every
baseline family needs a larger $k$ to catch up (Table~\ref{tab:coverage-trend}).

\textbf{Ablation: soft vs.\ hard coverage.} Finally, we check what the soft
coverage formulation itself is buying, independent of the greedy mechanism
around it. The discrete set-cover baseline (Section~\ref{sec:method:baselines})
uses the same lazy/CELF greedy mechanism and approximation guarantee as our
method, but a binary rather than $\lambda$-decayed coverage signal. It is
the slowest of all \emph{structured} methods to approach ceiling coverage:
it never reaches $\ge 0.999$ within the tested range (Table~\ref{tab:coverage-trend}),
behind every degree-based and centrality-based baseline as well as our own
method. This is a direct empirical confirmation that the soft,
distance-aware formulation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:coverage} buys something
concrete over classical hard-coverage greedy, not just a theoretical nicety.

The comparisons above all use a single representative $\lambda$ per family.
We now ask the question specific to our method: what does $\lambda$ itself
trade off? Table~\ref{tab:lambda-sweep} shows five metrics at one
representative $k$; the prose below states which patterns hold at that
single $k$ and which hold across the full sweep.

Checking every one of the 39 tested $k$ values, not just the $k=10{,}000$
snapshot in Table~\ref{tab:lambda-sweep}, three distinct patterns emerge:

\textbf{Monotonically improving toward $\lambda=1$ (100\% of $k$ values).}
Semantic coverage and unk rate improve monotonically as $\lambda$ increases
from $0.2$ to $1.0$, at every single tested $k$, always best at
$\lambda=1.0$. Uncovered rate follows the same direction but saturates to
exactly $0$ well before $\lambda=1.0$: by $\lambda \ge 0.6$ it is already
$0$ at every $k \ge 1{,}500$, and even $\lambda=0.2$ reaches $0$ by
$k=5{,}000$.

\textbf{Peaked at $\lambda \approx 0.8$ (large majority of $k$ values).} In
the other direction, distance score is maximized at $\lambda=0.8$ at
\emph{all} 39 tested $k$ values, dropping again at $\lambda=1.0$ every time
(e.g.\ $0.863 \to 0.728$ at $k=10{,}000$). The same peak shows up, slightly
less consistently, in three other metrics: tree complexity is minimized at
$\lambda=0.8$ at 38 of 39 $k$ values, conciseness at 34 of 39, and
uniqueness entropy at 30 of 39 ($\lambda=0.6$ is the usual runner-up). This
confirms your outline's prediction that these metrics trade off against raw
coverage -- and shows that $\lambda=0.8$ is consistently, not just
occasionally, the best single setting for this group.

\textbf{Its own pattern: exact rate.} Exact rate follows neither group
above. Among the positive $\lambda$ values, it is highest at $\lambda=0.2$
at every tested $k$, decreasing overall as $\lambda$ increases toward $1$ --
moving \emph{opposite} to semantic coverage and unk rate. The decrease is
not perfectly monotonic (at $k=10{,}000$, $\lambda=1.0$'s $0.185$ partially
rebounds above $\lambda=0.8$'s $0.155$), but $\lambda=0.2$ is, without
exception, the best positive-$\lambda$ choice for this metric.

\textbf{The degenerate case $\lambda=0$.} Setting $\lambda=0$ is not merely
a bad setting; Eq.~\eqref{eq:coverage} shows it is a qualitatively different
objective. At $\lambda=0$, every "otherwise" term vanishes, so
$S_h(u,T) = S_0(u,T) = \mathbf{1}[u \in T]$ at every horizon, and
$F_D(T)|_{\lambda=0}$ reduces exactly to $\text{exact\_rate}(M,T)$: selecting
at $\lambda=0$ optimizes exact-match rate, not coverage. The sweep confirms
this is not just an algebraic curiosity. Across all 39 $k$ values, the
$\lambda=0$ selection has \emph{strictly the highest} exact rate of every
tested $\lambda$ at every single $k$ (e.g.\ $0.217$ at $k=10{,}000$, vs.\
$0.199$ for the next best, $\lambda=0.2$) -- exactly as predicted, since
this is directly what $\lambda=0$ optimizes -- and, at the same time,
\emph{strictly the lowest} semantic coverage of every $\lambda$ at every
single $k$, dramatically so at small $k$: $0.012$ at $k=500$, actually
\emph{below} $k$-random's $0.050$ at the same budget, and still only
$0.491$ at the largest tested $k=19{,}500$ (vs.\ $\ge 0.999$ for every
$\lambda \ge 0.2$). \notetoself{note for precision: the
\emph{selection}-time objective at $\lambda=0$ literally equals
exact\_rate, but the reported \texttt{semantic\_coverage} column is always
\emph{evaluated} at $\lambda=1$ (Section~\ref{sec:method:metrics}'s
convention), so the two numbers are not the same quantity computed twice --
the point is that a token set optimized for exact\_rate performs
catastrophically under the standard $\lambda=1$ coverage evaluation
everyone else is judged by.}

\textbf{Practical takeaway.} Putting the four patterns together,
$\lambda \approx 0.8$ is a reasonable default when concise, nearby
representations matter as much as raw coverage: it costs essentially
nothing in semantic coverage or unk rate relative to $\lambda=1$ (both
already at or near ceiling by moderate $k$), while giving the best distance
score, tree complexity, and usually conciseness and discriminative power in
the whole sweep. $\lambda=1$ remains the right choice if raw coverage at the
smallest possible $k$ is the only thing that matters, or if a downstream use
case specifically wants the highest possible exact-match rate among
large-$\lambda$ settings. \doubt{"depends on personal choice" -- happy to
sharpen this into an explicit recommendation/decision rule if you tell me
what the downstream FM actually optimizes for (e.g.\ embedding-table size
vs.\ per-concept fidelity).}
