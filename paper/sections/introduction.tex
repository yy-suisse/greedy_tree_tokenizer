\subsection{Background}

\begin{quote}\small\itshape
\begin{itemize}
\item EHR Foundation Model (FM) arises nowaday, with potential to be assistive clinical decision tool
\item they often use standarized clinical vocabularies such as ICD, RXNORM, SNOMED, LONIC
\item standarized clinical vocabularies /ontologies DAG, are used to normalize clinical naming, and improve the interoperability
\item SNOMED CT is a DAG, comprehensive 400k + concepts (covering very broad clinical domains, diorders, medication, morphologies etc...) and semantically rich (composed by 100+ relationship types, have their own way to compose machine readable semantic expression), mappable to other clincial terminologies.
\end{itemize}
\end{quote}

Electronic health record (EHR) foundation models (FMs) are a recent class of
models that learn from large amounts of patient history, with the goal of
eventually assisting clinical decisions (e.g.\ Li et al., 2020, BEHRT; Yang
et al., 2023, TransformEHR). To train on data from many hospitals at once,
these models cannot rely on free text alone, because the same clinical fact
is written differently in different places. Instead, they are trained on
standardized clinical vocabularies -- ICD, RxNorm, SNOMED~CT, LOINC -- so
that the same fact always gets the same code
\citeneeded{standardized clinical vocabulary / interoperability reference}.

Several of these vocabularies are more than flat lists of codes: they are
ontologies, organized as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), in which a concept
is connected to other concepts that define it. SNOMED~CT is the largest and
most detailed example. It contains more than 400{,}000 concepts, covering
disorders, procedures, medications, and more. Its concepts are connected by
over 100 relationship types, so that each concept can be described as a
machine-readable combination of others, and it can be mapped onto most other
major clinical terminologies \citeneeded{SNOMED International concept model /
release statistics}. \doubt{worth cross-checking against Su et al. (2025,
MedTok)'s own count -- they report 303{,}325 SNOMED~CT concepts specifically
(out of 617{,}490 codes total across 8 systems: ICD-10-CM 81{,}184, RxNorm
81{,}151, ICD-10-PCS 61{,}644, NDC 54{,}560, ICD-9 18{,}365, CPT 10{,}602, ATC
6{,}659) -- likely a different SNOMED edition/extension than your ~400k
figure; worth a one-line reconciliation rather than leaving two different
SNOMED counts unexplained in the same paper.}

\subsection{Gap}

\begin{quote}\small\itshape
\begin{itemize}
\item training FM needs either large amount of patient data (but patient data is difficult to get)
\item or need adequate size of clinical vocabularies. (but medical vocabularies are large size)
\item Current methods are:
\item (cite) LLM byte-pair on categorical medical concept: data-driven, no semantic preservation, highly dependant to data quality and cpncept orders.
\item (cite) keep highest prevalent top k concepts, highest entropy contribution top k concepts: data-driven, loss of considerable clinical information.
\item (cite) troncate/reduce leaf concepts to their parents concepts: data-driven, loss of medical granularity.
\item (cite) or just keep all concepts as it is, if we have lots of patient data. but since concept usage distribution are usually long tail, many rare concepts can not be learnt in a proper way.
\item (cite) learnable embedding space codebook: loss of controillability and interpretability
\item all of above mentioned methods are ignoring the semantic richness of clinical vocabulary DAG when try to deal with vocabulary size.
\end{itemize}
\end{quote}

This scale creates a problem for training an EHR FM. Learning a good
representation for every one of these concepts takes a large amount of
patient data, and such data is expensive and often hard to access
\doubt{do you want to cite/quantify this, e.g. typical cohort sizes needed vs.
available, or leave it as a general statement?}. Reducing the vocabulary to a
size the available data can actually support does not avoid the problem
either: concept usage in real EHR data follows a long tail, so most concepts
are already too rare to learn well, no matter how the vocabulary is chosen.
Su et al. (2025, MedTok) report the same pattern independently -- "low-frequency
codes suffer from poor embeddings, reducing performance on underrepresented
conditions" -- though, like us, without an actual frequency plot to back it
up. \doubt{would strengthen this sentence a lot to show your own long-tail
plot from the mapped-concept usage data, if you have it, rather than relying
on two qualitative claims (yours and MedTok's) with no numbers behind either.}

Several strategies already exist for shrinking the vocabulary, and
Table~\ref{tab:related-approaches} summarizes them side by side. Byte-pair
and other subword encodings merge concepts by co-occurrence statistics alone
(Sennrich et al., 2016; Kudo \& Richardson, 2018, SentencePiece): they need
no ontology, but the resulting vocabulary depends heavily on data quality and
on the arbitrary order in which concepts appear. Keeping only the most
frequent, or highest-information, concepts (top-$k$ pruning) drops a
significant amount of clinically relevant information simply because it is
rare \citeneeded{prevalence/entropy-based vocabulary pruning in clinical ML}.
Rolling leaf concepts up to their ontology parent shrinks the vocabulary but
loses clinical detail: a specific diagnosis collapses into a broader one
\citeneeded{ontology roll-up / ICD truncation approach}. Keeping the full
vocabulary avoids all of these losses, but only postpones the long-tail
problem to the model, and only works if enough data is available
\doubt{is there a citation you have in mind for this "no reduction" baseline,
or is it meant as the trivial comparison point rather than a cited method?}.
Finally, learnable embedding-space codebooks compress the vocabulary without
any hand-written rules (Van den Oord et al., 2017), but the resulting tokens
are then just indices into a learned space: they no longer correspond to
concepts a person can recognize or check.

Table~\ref{tab:related-approaches} makes the gap concrete: no existing
approach combines the two properties we want -- using the DAG's structure
\emph{and} producing tokens a person can inspect. \doubt{Is the sharpest way
to state the gap "none of these use the DAG structure itself," or the
stronger claim "none of these produce a token set that is both semantically
motivated \emph{and} inspectable/controllable"? The second framing lines up
more directly with the contribution list below and with the table -- your
call on which to lead with.}

\textbf{Closest related work.} One method in the table needs a closer look,
because it is the exception to "no graph structure": Su et al. (2025,
MedTok) feed each code's neighborhood in a biomedical knowledge graph, along
with its text description, into a multimodal encoder, then compress the
result into a shared, learned codebook. So the real difference between our
method and MedTok is not "uses the graph" versus "does not" -- it is
\emph{how}. MedTok's tokens are indices into a continuous space learned by a
neural network: there is no way to look at a token and say which
relationships in the graph produced it. Our tokens, by contrast, are chosen
directly from the graph by a deterministic rule (Section~\ref{sec:method}),
and every token a concept resolves to comes with an explicit record of which
relationships were followed to reach it. \doubt{this paragraph is my
synthesis of the distinction, not something either paper states outright --
worth double-checking it reads as fair to MedTok (it's a real, well-evaluated
method, not a strawman) rather than as dismissive.}

\subsection{Our method and contribution}

\begin{quote}\small\itshape
\begin{itemize}
\item applicable to DAG knowledge graph.
\item tokens selection: Represent concepts by smaller vocabulary size using greedy token selection approach
\item way to tokenize: Represent concepts by a tree/subgraph preserving the structure of the concepts the semantic value.
\item By apply to SNOMED CT concepts used in our local institut real data, we show our method can control how we tokenize and with what we tokenize (controllability and interpretability)
\item (maybe) if any mapping inter-ontology exist, we can use concepts in 1 ontology to represent/tokenize concepts in the other ontologies (interoperability)
\end{itemize}
\end{quote}

Building on this gap, we propose a method for selecting and building tokens
directly on a concept DAG such as SNOMED~CT. It has two parts. First, we
choose a small set of tokens with a greedy algorithm that maximizes how well
they cover the meaning of the target concepts (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
This lets us fix the vocabulary size in advance, instead of it falling out
of a training run. Second, instead of representing a concept as an unordered
set of the tokens it maps to, we represent it as a \emph{context tree}: a
small subgraph that records which relationship led to each token. This
matters in practice -- for example, a token for "kidney" and a token for
"left" stay attached to the same anatomical-site branch, instead of being
mixed up with an unrelated "right adrenal gland" branch found elsewhere in
the same concept \doubt{this kidney/adrenal example is carried over from
your existing PDF draft -- keep it, or swap in a real example from the
HUG-mapped concepts once we have one handy?}.

We apply this method to the SNOMED~CT concepts mapped by our institution's
semantician team. The result, unlike every approach in
Table~\ref{tab:related-approaches} including MedTok, is directly
controllable and inspectable: for any concept, a domain expert can see
exactly which relationships were used to reach which token.
